9.1 Lecture: what an LLM really is, and four modes of use

  • The deck builds on Ferrara's NBER paper (WP 35374, a practitioner's guide to LLMs/GenAI in economic-history research) — evidence that researchers without specialist skills can now do this work, e.g. parsing errors cut from 40% to under 1%.
  • An LLM is essentially a context-dependent next-word predictor. Statistical language models → neural networks → kernel smoothing (weaving in surrounding context, as in moving averages) — connected back to last year's spatio-temporal statistics seminar, and contrasted with 1980s knowledge engineering (if-then-else expert systems).
  • Misuse stems from anthropomorphizing. Treating it strictly as a word predictor makes it dramatically more usable.
  • Four modes of use: ① chat window (most common); ② IDE-integrated assistant (e.g. inside RStudio); ③ agentic (Claude Code — reads/writes files and executes; usable as a research assistant); ④ API mode (you write the loop in R etc., calling the LLM only where meaning must be interpreted). The API mode is a revolution — "programs become usable as data," leveraging 70–80 years of accumulated software.
  • Strengths: generating structured data, reading meaning from unstructured data, classifying free text. In an experiment extracting emotions from 630k paintings (post-1400), ChatGPT got 7 of 8 test images right; a job that took 2+ GPU-hours finished in 8 minutes. But questions, judgment, and writing stay with the researcher.
  • Weaknesses: hallucination, literature search, historical facts/data lookup, spatial reasoning, causal-inference design, regression variable selection, noisy generated proxies — plus the erosion of one's own judgment when the AI does too much thinking (cf. Berkeley's blanket AI ban; criticism of letting AI write theses).

9.2 Loop-engineering exercise — participants' practice

  • Design your repetitive work as a loop with the LLM inside; run it small, verify, improve (Andrew Ng). Discussion built on the Lynch / Ng pre-reads, with each participant bringing what they "repeatedly fix or repeatedly re-instruct."
  • Paper-processing loop (Kota Suzuki): one command that ① converts a paper PDF to Markdown, ② files it, ③ renames it, ④ generates a Japanese summary. Text conversion is the key step (PDFs get misread; adding a vision library improves accuracy). "Work I used to hand to first-to-third-year juniors now runs on this loop."
  • Financial models (Miyatake): calculations with determinate answers can be delegated; creative judgments (how to wire items into the P&L or cash flow) break down. An "external feedback loop" — having a separate expert review the agent's output — works well.
  • A thesis-research cautionary tale: agentic tools can implement a top-journal-grade model even at 5% understanding; chaining Claude → Codex cross-checks → further reviews can produce a finished artifact "without knowing where it went."
  • Limits: LLMs handle common-denominator content but cannot follow papers introducing genuinely new concepts — you still read the original yourself.
  • The criterion is cheap verification: don't ask what LLMs are good at — loop-ify the tasks in your own work that are cheapest to verify, and gains follow reliably.

9.3 Real options and "learning" as a new kind of uncertainty

  • The professor is preparing next month's Real Options Society lecture. Real options rest on irreversibility, uncertainty, and flexibility (the value of waiting); uncertainty comes in market, game-theoretic, and technical flavors.
  • The new insight: technical uncertainty is now bound up with learning. We have moved from "you don't know until you try" to "you don't know even after you try" — the LLM returns an answer whose correctness you cannot verify.
  • Learning, question-crafting, and investment in knowledge may form a framework unifying real options, VC, and innovation. Emphasis: "the machine cannot craft the question — that is the human's job."

9.4 The economics of AI (Miyatake's discussion note)

  • AI use splits into ① efficiency gains in existing work and ② creation of new demand/supply; today it is mostly ①, so are we stuck in zero-sum? (A discussion file prepared with Claude was shared.)
  • Paths beyond zero-sum: real-income gains from falling supply costs, Jevons' paradox, democratization of professional services (healthcare, legal), and non-market consumer surplus from free AI.
  • Efficiency gains may favor shareholders over employees. In Japan, adoption is back-office-centric; front-line use is limited to parts of manufacturing AX. Real estate's front line resists AI partly because IoT/sensing lags.
  • Versus the IT bubble: that era was software-only; this one entails physical investment (data centers, hyperscalers investing beyond private-sector scale). Investment in intangibles/knowledge is a new frontier — the concept of investment itself is shifting.
  • Democratization of software: at one real-estate firm, non-technical back-office staff drove remarkable results operating their PCs in natural language — the real revolution is non-programmers wielding programs without training.

9.5 Security risks

  • Agentic AI auto-installs libraries and carries real risk on corporate/financial-institution endpoints. A real case was shared of a Claude Code user accidentally publishing a client-data environment to GitHub.
  • Mitigations: sandboxed environments with confined permissions; separating closed internal-data chat from externally connected environments; some institutions allow only restricted environments. Also the structural worry: "I cannot myself verify whether the security officer's judgment is right."
  • A failure story: a Google Meet recording kept stopping at 10 minutes; LLMs couldn't solve it — it was a single setting. Where UIs change frequently, LLM knowledge is stale; prompts like "read today's documentation before answering" help.

9.6 Lunch conversation (excerpts)

  • IT bubble vs. AI boom: the internet delivered a genuinely new experience (the death of distance); where is AI's equivalent new industry? Also discussed: the railway-company analogy (infrastructure + real estate + operations) applied to data centers, and their much larger tenant risk. A leading candidate for the October symposium's key question.
  • AI and social structure: concentration of wealth, basic-income scenarios, and the risk that money pools with US hyperscalers/labs so Japan cannot fund the transfers. Counterpoints: cost savings that expand output can raise overall welfare, and blue-collar wages are rising; the graver problem may be the loss of training opportunities for juniors.
  • An expanding concept of investment: the professor flagged a recent NBER paper (extending investment/real-options thinking) as having "the same impact as encountering real options 30 years ago," and intends to build next month's Real Options Society lecture around it — details to be shared with the seminar after the lecture. A candidate for the seminar's next signature theme now that the LLM lecture series has reached a milestone.
  • Governance talk: the practical limits of outside directors (monthly board packets can't reveal what's inside), D&O insurance caps versus mega-suits, and how organized fraud (e.g. circular trading) is nearly undetectable from outside — ultimately reliant on whistleblowers.
  • Markets: real rates still negative despite the BOJ's hiking trend; yen weakness and FX-asset taxation; the maxim "buy real estate in bad times, hold back in good times" and worries about post-crash recovery under population decline.
  • Plus: Son Masayoshi's semi-autobiography (assessed as a financial investor), the Silicon Valley ecosystem, space development, the strength of Japanese pop culture, and WBS alumni founders.
