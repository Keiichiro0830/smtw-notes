Background paper — planning capital and staged discovery
Caplin (2026), “Planning Capital and Discovery-Based Learning-by-Doing”, PDF Original (NBER Working Paper No. 35349)

Shared by Professor Kawaguchi (Andrew Caplin, New York University; NBER Working Paper No. 35349, June 2026). The paper treats frontier investment as staged discovery: firms move through sequences of costly inquiry stages, choosing what to ask, embodying the question in a costly action, and interpreting the answer. The accumulated diagnostic understanding is itself the asset, which the paper calls planning capital. Chips, fabs, data centres, cloud platforms and power commitments are valued not only for the capacity already in place but because they carry traces of past learning and generate more of it. This is the background to the afternoon discussion on 22 August about hyperscaler valuation and diagnostic uncertainty, and to the 3 October symposium. NBER working papers are pre-review discussion drafts; copyright rests with the author (© 2026 Andrew Caplin).

What the day covers — an “introductory tutorial in agentic spatio-temporal statistics”

Prof. Kawaguchi shared the design on August 1 and confirmed it on August 5. The framing is an introduction to spatio-temporal statistics (the statistics of dependence) as the foundation for understanding the LLM trilogy. Two pillars: (1) a review of the key points of the 2025 exercises (some participants did not take them), and (2) agentic work with Claude Code (new). The “statistical modelling loop” is treated as two loops — the human thinking loop supported by AI chat, and the loop Claude Code runs autonomously — implemented on each participant’s own PC.

In the professor’s words, “you will hardly need to write any R code yourself”. Humans concentrate on judgement; the machine handles execution.

On August 5 the professor sharpened the aim further: “if the point were to add six hours’ worth of spatio-temporal statistics to your knowledge, you would be far better off reading a book. Instead I want you to experience searching for what lies behind real data.” This is not a day for accumulating knowledge but for working backwards from the goal and experiencing the search. The intended result is precisely to locate the boundary — how far the AI takes you, and where your own statistical understanding has to start.

The professor’s note: “an age that needs upstream, not downstream, experts” (August 1)

On August 1 the professor sent a PDF making the aim of the day concrete. The conclusion is on the last page.

Everyone,

Last night’s email was abstract, so I am attaching something concrete.

The attached PDF should make clear that, with “the expert plus the machine”, the non-expert (the assistant) is no longer needed.

“As mechanisation advances this fast, what should one study in order to become an expert in statistical modelling?”

I think education as we have known it is being forced into a great change. Past academic credentials may mean almost nothing. That is my honest impression from putting the attachment together. (Prof. Kawaguchi, August 1)

The content is a record of Claude Code being asked to do question 3 from the 2025 exercises — estimating model fm1 on the Boston housing data by weighted least squares. Moran’s I stays significant after WLS and in fact grows, and the OLS and WLS coefficients diverge systematically on RM, NOX, LSTAT and AGE — a problem in how the mean is specified, which no amount of variance correction will remove.

The conclusion on the last page. Take statistical modelling as four steps — (1) write the model, (2) fit it to the data, (3) test it, (4) judge whether the model is sound. Steps 2 and 3 simply “get done” if you hand them to Claude and R. The human concentrates on 1 and 4 — and that is where deep knowledge, and experience, are required.

From the August 5 demo — what to watch for

At the organising meeting the professor demonstrated Claude Code running OLS and WLS on the Boston housing data. Three things worth knowing in advance.

You do not have to launch RStudio

Claude Code calls R directly and returns the result, so you need R itself but not the RStudio window — that is how the professor actually works. In the professor’s words: “when I did exactly what Claude told me, I never used RStudio; it was just sitting there.” Having both installed is still the safest setup, so the request stays R + RStudio.

The human role shifts towards design

Choosing the model, reading the residuals and deciding which model to try next stay with the human; running the code goes to Claude Code. The professor’s own slides split it that way — human on the left, Claude Code on the right.

The limits of the AI are themselves the topic

Can it read the residuals of a linear regression and decide whether to add spatial or temporal structure? That was debated at the meeting. The professor: “I don’t know whether it will give the right answer, and it may be lying. Let’s try it and discuss.” Finding the boundary — how far it goes and where the human is needed — is itself the output of the day.

The professor also noted that “vibe statistical modeling” has become a talking point in English-language statistics teaching, with comparative studies of general-purpose AI (Claude / Gemini / ChatGPT) against statistics-specific tools, and plans to cover this on the day.
