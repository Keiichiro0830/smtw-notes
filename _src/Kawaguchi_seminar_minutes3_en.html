8.1 Context & recap

  • 1st session held May 9; a ~40-page "LLM prompt engineering" deck circulated May 12 (the professor described "brain fry" from over-loading knowledge). Cadence: monthly, 2-hour slots.
  • The professor's LLM trilogy (interpreting LLMs through the lens of statistical models) is distributed via a ¥500/month newsletter. An "AI practical-use guide" by a member was also shared.
  • Flow of the day: members first shared reactions to the pre-reads (Wolfram / Chollet / the LLM trilogy / mental models); the professor then unpacked "what an LLM is" in order — mechanism, history, finance.
  • Professor: "The Nikkei writes for middle-schoolers; corporate AI use today is middle-school level. What's needed is understanding the mechanism." Stanford's ~1000-page NLP text is free online — "now that LLMs translate, 'it's in English' is no excuse."

8.2 Current state of AI & impact on practice

  • Given compute / data limits, controlling the vector space via prompts is the optimal use today.
  • Bloomberg: an ex-SoftBank AI lead runs AI training for financial firms — ¥1–2M per talk, 3 talks/day, booked out 2 months. The competitive axis has shifted from "understanding" to "usage."
  • Hard-to-verify deliverables (Sasaki): others' AI output hides their reasoning, making errors hard to catch and adding work. Best used as an extension of your own ability, not for things you know nothing about.
  • ~80% of LLM output is reliable (Iinuma), but it breaks down on hard problems; copy-paste submission leaves understanding shallow.
  • Sycophancy risk: RL optimizes for answers users like, so errors get flagged less the more you rely on it — linked to "brain fry." A Harvard study suggests humans stop thinking during AI use.
  • ChatGPT → child-services case: a domestic consultation was routed straight to "call child services," ending in an outcome no one wanted — raising how the young should engage with LLMs.

8.3 Anthropomorphism & the ELIZA effect

  • Hard to escape the anthropomorphism trap — nicknaming the model, writing polite-form prompts (Suzuki). The ELIZA effect (feeling personhood in your interlocutor) is noted by IBM; experts must handle it structurally.
  • Hypothesis on binary/machine-language prompting: clearer instructions but fewer of the emergent good answers natural language yields (a Cornell paper).
  • An LLM is of a kind with a self-estimated regression model — treat it as a tool with known flaws.

8.4 How LLMs work (Prof. Kawaguchi)

  • At core an autoregressive (AR) model — "a machine that probabilistically predicts the next word," an extension of regression.
  • "You can't use what you can't build": tracing the history (Hinton 1986 → Bengio 2003), the professor sets the bar for an expert as "give me funding and engineers and I could build something better than today's AI."
  • The breakthrough is embedding: integers alone carry no relatedness, so words become ~768-dim vectors and similarity is read off the inner product (sentiment, gender, emotion…).
  • An entry analogy is the text-mining dictionary (tagging words by sentiment / gender). But in practice features aren't hand-set — learned by backprop from random init, hence a black box. Feature extraction (the pre-processing) is the crux of ML, and it is distinct from the AR computation inside the Transformer (a confusion the professor corrected).
  • Transformer = AR + kernel smoothing: kernel regression injects context (dependency) into Markov-style independence. The professor reached this from kernel smoothing independently, later finding an equivalent paper (Tsai et al. 2019).
  • Theoretical basis: universal function approximation (White's theorem); learning = information compression (the human brain does the same). History: Hinton 1986 → AI "died" in the 90s → Bengio et al. 2003 neural LM was the turning point.

8.5 Mechanistic interpretability & emergence — Wolfram vs. Chollet

  • Mechanistic interpretability: a new field probing parameter-activation patterns to see where things happen inside the net.
  • Emergence: thought-like phenomena may arise incidentally from vector-space relations — "stochastic parrot or emergence?" is under active empirical research.
  • Wolfram: prompt engineering is limited because the computation is a black box ("everything is computation"); Chollet: controllable via vector-space operations — the key contrast.
  • Intelligence may sit more in embedding (feature extraction) than the net itself. Scaling laws: pushing neuron counts toward brain scale produced unexpected performance (reason unknown).
  • AI agents are "just a program with an LLM mixed in"; physical AI (robots, self-driving) won't run on a language model alone — reasoning beyond language needs a computer-science foundation (the professor frames himself as "computational science, not computational linguistics").

8.6 Mental models & the scholar-practitioner

  • Mental model = "how you interpret the world." Charlie Munger's latticework of multiple models; a 20-year theme for the professor (rooted in translating The Science of Investing).
  • Scholar-practitioner: able to restate practical knowledge in academic language; business school exists to change students' mental models.
  • AI has no mental model — grounding the role left to humans.
  • Three learning stages: memorize → diagram → create (no skipping memorization). LLM pre-training is information compression (Wolfram: "learning = compression"). The professor's mentor: "think for yourself before reading the textbook."

8.7 Financial markets & AI use

  • What stays human: ①setting direction ②taking responsibility (Suzuki).
  • Temperature: low for law/contracts/regulation, high for ideation and uncertain forecasts.
  • LLM stock prediction underperforms a simple AR model (language vs. financial features differ); useful for DD documents but hard to outperform (a semi-efficient market erases dependencies). Conversely, rebuilding the financial feature extraction (embedding) could yield a predictive model.
  • Edge = not-yet-public primary information (monitoring). Anecdotes: the professor on a Mori Building private-fund investment committee; ex-Goldman CEO Blankfein "just phones friends" post-retirement.
  • On ChatGPT confidence levels: low-coverage names had the highest Sharpe — active small-cap strategies favor low coverage.

8.8 Macro & market specifics

  • Memory-chip high P/E: structurally low-P/E names trading at 50–60× on a "earn it all in 2–3 years" bet; managers disagree.
  • Semiconductor capex framed as the inverse of a real option (charge in rather than wait — a gold rush).
  • BOJ: Gov. Ueda's June 3 talk — rate hikes plus tapering/halting JGB purchases to manage supply-demand; US Treasury Sec. Bessent's late-May visit read as nudging hikes.
  • TSMC Kumamoto: read less as geopolitics than "the fastest place in Japan to build a fab → 2–3-year payback."
  • Big-picture (professor): government / Keidanren / BOJ aligned to inflate away the relative value of public debt — a bet that breaks if ~¥1,000T of household assets flee. J-REITs: good fundamentals but abandoned on supply-demand.

8.9 Regress-PCA vs. embedding (research idea)

  • PCA / regress-PCA compress (reduce dimensions); a language model does the opposite — 768 dimensions per word (expansion). Connects to last year's spatio-temporal-statistics regress-PCA.
  • Professor: "'add dimensions, add a neural net, and it works' is interesting as research" — but uninterpretable, so better for small-cap / hedge funds than asset managers. Vectorizing economic variables plus the prevailing narrative could, in principle, beat AR.

8.11 Dinner ① — new real-estate assets & investing under inflation

  • Student-housing business (Ushare): rolled out near Waseda; shared space on the ground floor plus mid-floor commons. The founder, inspired by a Harvard real-estate-investing book, left Kajima and funded a Harvard degree through his own property investments; gathers strong international students salon-style and supports their job hunting.
  • A gap in J-REITs: no student-housing-focused J-REIT exists. Partnering with top universities could make it an asset class that works even under inflation; domestic core investors (fixed yields) can't beat inflation, leaving room for foreign value-add entry.
  • Fandom economy: 10,000 high-intensity people beat 1,000,000 low-intensity ones — gather "those who get it" (an idea that also echoes this seminar).
  • Investing under inflation: property analysts' rent forecasts have badly missed — the cause is confusing nominal and real values (a blind spot for generations who never lived through inflation).
  • Cycle view: the post-COVID recovery (~72 months) may turn over around summer. Some see PE-fund-style asset inflation "about to end," while others see a "Reiwa bubble" as post-war surplus capital flows into property. The next bubble's energy: AI / compute / data centers (Amazon's and Google's out-of-core capex are the tell).

8.12 Dinner ② — the BOJ, the newsletter, and AI-assisted article analysis

  • BOJ independence: now effectively the government's "house bank," functioning to support inflation-as-taxation — hence the value of a continuous "BOJ watcher."
  • Using AI to find errors in Nikkei articles: feed an article and ask it to "find the mistakes." But the model can "flatter" the Nikkei and just praise it, so prompt design (supply correct facts first, then compare/evaluate) is key.
  • Nominal vs. real error: an FT column compared equity and bond yields in nominal terms — the claim is right but the method wrong; such analyses make good newsletter material.
  • Newsletter operations: a broad readership makes calibrating difficulty hard. Content candidates: "BOJ watcher," "errors in Nikkei articles," "real-estate investing under inflation." Also practiced: writing market analysis as replies to industry contacts and watching the reaction.

8.13 Dinner ③ — geopolitics, war, Taiwan, China

  • War as "fireworks": a way to "burn off" siphoned surplus capital. Short of nuclear use the victory condition is never met, so "firing the missile" becomes the end in itself — part of the same hunt for "exits for money" (green / DX → AI).
  • Taiwan / TSMC: as long as TSMC is in Taiwan, a Chinese military move is too economically damaging to risk (the leading-edge nodes are concentrated there).
  • China's internal dynamics: a power structure where the military periodically slips control; the navy is said to still carry a grudge from the First Sino-Japanese War; a Party official reportedly told a Japanese business leader "the military won't listen to us."
  • Japan's SDF accumulates realistic training through hundreds of annual scrambles, while actual shoot-downs are deterred by mutual face. After any war's end, China's surplus capital could flow into property and inflate a bubble.
  • Aside (link to investing): from tales of petty fraud and romance scams — "training to spot market distortions resembles a fraudster's thinking," and "the deceiver is more serious (they can't eat if they fail), so the contest is already decided." Closing consensus: in the AI era, face-to-face dialogue is precious.
