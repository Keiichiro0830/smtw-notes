English summary (working translation by K. Matsumae)

Framing. This document is the successor to the two manual-style guides Prof. Kawaguchi circulated earlier ("AI for business professionals" and "AI for data analysts"). Those were written by Claude as practical manuals. This one is a step deeper — an attempt to share what an LLM-literate expert is actually doing in their head when they prompt a model.

Method. The professor started with a Google search seeded by "Chollet 2023," then snowballed through citations and ran a multi-turn Claude dialogue to assemble a five-era history of LLM research:

  • Era 1 — Roots of distributed representation: Hinton (1986) → Bengio (2003) neural language model → Mikolov (2013) Word2Vec → Sutskever/Cho (2014) Seq2Seq → Bahdanau (2015) Attention → Vaswani (2017) Transformer.
  • Era 2 — Pretraining revolution: BERT (2018), GPT-2 (2019).
  • Era 3 — Scaling: Kaplan et al. (2020) scaling laws, GPT-3 (2020).
  • Era 4 — Alignment: Chain-of-Thought (2022), InstructGPT / RLHF (2022).
  • Era 5 — Theoretical integration: Chollet (2023), "LLM = a huge vector space; prompt = a path-specification inside that space."

Chollet, paragraph by paragraph. The latter half of the document reads François Chollet's "How I think about LLM prompt engineering" passage by passage, after a primer on Word2Vec (co-occurrence → distance conversion). Chollet's claim, which Kawaguchi endorses: an LLM is essentially Word2Vec scaled up, sharing four properties — (i) it is an embedding device; (ii) it relies on the distributional hypothesis; (iii) it uses cosine-style distance; (iv) it lives in a 10³–10⁴ dimensional space — plus Self-Attention as the addition that makes it dynamic.

Author's message. "From p.4 onward you can see the picture I have inside my own PC when I use an LLM." Bengio (2003) — words as feature vectors — is presented as a continuation of Prof. Kawaguchi's earlier teaching series "Introduction to Spatial Statistics": same mathematical move (objects → vectors → distance), different domain.

Source: Kawaguchi_20260512_LLM_Prompt_Engineering.docx. Summary prepared 2026-05-13 — please refer to the DOCX for the original Japanese text and Claude-dialogue transcripts.

How LLMs work (Prof. Kawaguchi's newsletter supplement series)

Circulated 2026-05-30 – 06-04 (JP). A serialized technical explainer Prof. Kawaguchi wrote to share "a mental image of what the LLM is doing inside your own PC." It builds up from embeddings → autoregressive next-token prediction → the attention mechanism, landing on a statistical reading — "attention = kernel smoothing" (Tsai et al. 2019). It situates the Transformer as a continuation of spatial statistics / kernel methods, and forms the theoretical backbone of the 3rd meeting.

Prof. Kawaguchi original · circulated 2026-05-30 – 06-04 (3 parts, JP)
Yuichiro Kawaguchi — How Large Language Models Work (1)–(3)

(1) Word vectorization (embeddings) — what it means to map text into numeric vectors. (2) Neural probabilistic language model — formalizing next-token prediction as a product of autoregressive conditional probabilities, after Bengio et al. (2003). (3) Attention & the Transformer — recasting the limits of the fixed-window FFN as kernel smoothing (a conditional expectation as a weighted average), and connecting it to attention.

Academic source · EMNLP 2019
Tsai, Bai, Yamada, Morency & Salakhutdinov — Transformer Dissection: A Unified Understanding of Transformer's Attention via the Lens of Kernel

The academic grounding for the "attention = kernel smoothing" reading in newsletter (3). It shows the Transformer's self-attention can be understood uniformly as a kernel-weighted average (kernel smoothing) — the primary source that directly links Prof. Kawaguchi's kernel-method / spatial-statistics arguments to LLMs.

Mental models (the foundation of intellectual training)

"The more experienced the expert, the more firmly they are anchored to an unconscious mental model — a fixed way of thinking — that surfaces as intuition." These materials treat, through the lens of education theory, the very thing this seminar seeks to question. Anchored on Plotnick et al. (2026) and its constructivist framework FRAME, with a Japanese translation, commentary, and learning routines.

Paper · 2026
Plotnick et al. — Between Knowing and Becoming: A Constructivist Framework to Bridge Theory and Practice in Leadership

Proposes FRAME, a teaching framework that makes unconscious decision-making (mental models) conscious and articulable, moving learners from "reproductive thinking" toward transformative learning by deliberately inducing cognitive disequilibrium to update entrenched patterns of thought.
