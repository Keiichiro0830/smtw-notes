How August 22 connects — the theme is “learning”

The August 22 workshop is the groundwork for this symposium. In the August 1 email the professor wrote: “on that basis, in October we will be discussing a new concept of investment at the symposium”. On August 5 the connection was put into words.

“The trial and error on August 22 — trying a model, deciding it isn’t this one, isn’t that one — and the problem a firm faces are an analogy. In both cases the final goal is invisible. That is why the new concept of investment is not the financial engineering we were taught; it has learning in it. The whole point of the August 22 experience is for you to feel how you learn your way towards a structure you cannot see.” (Prof. Kawaguchi, August 5)

So August 22 is the experience of hunting for the invisible structure behind data and October 3 is the argument about how a firm approaches an invisible future — the same structure seen twice, joined at the point of “how one approaches the invisible”, which is learning. The question we have carried since April — how the IT bubble and the AI boom differ, and how they are the same — is to be brought to a conclusion from that angle.

The professor’s angle

Plenty of analysts write about “the IT boom versus the AI boom”; the professor’s angle is not theirs. The July 22 explanation comes down to four points.

1. A generational change in valuation method

During the IT boom, DCF was not enough for a loss-making Amazon, and papers appeared arguing it could be valued with real options (modelling even the drift term as a stochastic process). In today’s AI boom, real options alone cannot handle hyperscaler valuations; you have to reach for planning capital (cognitive economics).

2. A paradigm shift in learning

As the cost of obtaining an answer collapsed, the value moved to constructing the sequence of questions.

3. The economics of platforms

During the IT boom, the economics of platforms was simply not visible.

4. A view of history as a chain of bubbles

The IT boom killed a great many ventures and the economy worsened. The US then propped it up with a housing bubble, which went too far and became the global financial crisis. The next bubble was to be raised in green and IT — which is where we are now.
